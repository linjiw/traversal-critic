The World Model as Judge

Toward humanoid navigation in cluttered, occupied indoor spaces: a video world model scores the robot's behavior at training time — and never deploys. Current stage: the learned judge works; physics-in-the-loop training is next.

In 30 seconds: Humanoids can walk now — but they still can't cross a cluttered living room with people in it. The blocker isn't balance; it's that nobody can write down the reward for "squeeze through that gap politely, duck under the table, don't crowd the person." So we don't write it — we learn it from a video foundation model. We fine-tune a video-language world model into a traversal critic that watches a rollout clip and scores it 1–5, then use that score as the reward for training a small navigation policy over a frozen whole-body controller. Nothing big deploys on the robot — and the critic's judgment transfers to real robot video.

The measured contribution today is the critic (a learned judge of traversal quality, r = 0.53 on held-out scenes, ~71% of oracle shaping recovered). The robot capability — actually walking across cluttered, narrow homes — is the goal, not the current status: our best policy reaches 39% held-out success in this simplified setting. The approach: judge, not simulator, not policy

Video world models are used as simulators (imagine futures) or policies (VLAs) — both fight the models' weakness (long-horizon generation) and put billions of parameters near the control loop. We use the third option: judging, which is one forward pass, asynchronous, and training-time only.

1 — The problem is the reward.

Traversal quality in occupied clutter is perceptual and unwritable. Our own hand-crafted baseline reward silently collapsed PPO to standing still — the exact brittleness we're removing.

2 — Judging fits the model.

Recognition is easier than generation. Scoring "was that safe and natural?" takes one VLM pass over a short clip, not thirty diffusion steps per imagined frame.

3 — Supervision is free.

Privileged simulator state (clearance, contacts, person distance) converts to ordinal labels deterministically. ~6,000 clips labeled, zero human annotations — and the critic generalizes the judgment to pixels.

What the critic learns to judge

Procedurally generated cluttered houses — narrow doorway gaps (0.6–1.2 m), low tables, furniture, moving people — traversed by scripted policies spanning five quality bands. The auto-labeler scores each episode on safety, clearance, motion quality, and social compliance, with safety as a hard ceiling.

New: real contact dynamics (physics-in-the-loop)

The full released SONIC stack — kinematic planner → motion-token encoder → decoder → PD torques — now runs under MuJoCo contact physics in our cluttered scenes. Clutter pushes back, falls are real, and the mode decision (walk vs. crawl) has physical consequences. These clips are scripted routes with a fixed mode rule, not the learned policy: they demonstrate the environment and control stack, and are the substrate the reward-learning comparison is being re-run on.

Paper

The World Model as Judge: Learned Traversal Rewards for Humanoid Navigation in Cluttered, Occupied Spaces

Humanoid robots can walk, but they cannot yet traverse the narrow, cluttered, human-occupied spaces where they are meant to work. The obstacle is not low-level control but reward specification: behaviors such as turning shoulders through a tight gap, crouching under furniture, or yielding to a person resist hand-crafted reward engineering, and their quality is inherently perceptual. We propose using a video-language world model as a learned reward model. We fine-tune a compact (2B) video-language model into a traversal critic that scores short navigation clips on collision safety, clearance management, motion quality, and social compliance, supervised by privileged simulator state automatically converted to ordinal labels — no human annotation. The critic then provides reward shaping for training a lightweight vision-based navigation policy that commands a frozen, pretrained whole-body controller; the world model is used at training time only, leaving deployment unencumbered. On held-out scenes, the fine-tuned critic predicts ground-truth traversal quality from pixels alone (Pearson r = 0.53 vs. 0.15 near-base on a single fixed yardstick), improving monotonically with data scale, class balancing, and viewpoint quality, and transfers to real robot video without collapse (n=15 real clips, all positive).
